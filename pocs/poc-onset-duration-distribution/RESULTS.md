Source: `Mr Blue Sky — segment 00:05.00–05:10.37` (audio `a1689618…`, 305.37 s).
2398 transcribed events → **1432 right-hand onsets** → **719 attacks** after the
20 ms chord collapse. Hand labelling matched every single event (1432 R + 966 L =
2398, zero unmatched, zero out of range).

Split at **217.0 s** (3:37 on the segment clock).

## 1. Headline

**The approach works, and it works better than expected on segment A.**

Segment A's 528 gaps do not spread out — half of them (50.2 %) fall inside a
single 273–423 ms basin whose median is **337.2 ms**, and 92 % of all gaps sit
inside one of six peaks. That is a crisp ladder, not a smear. Divide the peaks by
335 ms and every one of them lands on a printable figure ratio, five of the six
within 5.4 %.

Segment B is a different distribution — different dominant peak, different shape
— so the method does detect the change. But B is only 189 gaps of mostly running
notes, and it runs straight into the one ambiguity this method cannot resolve
alone (§4).

## 4. Interpretation

### 4a. Segment A is not binary — it is swung, at 1.70 : 1

The ladder is generated by **two** intervals, 211 and 125 ms, whose **sum is
exactly the dominant peak** (211 + 125 = 336 ≈ 337). Everything else is a beat
multiple plus one of them:

```
125            = short half of a swung beat
211            = long half
337 = 211+125  = one beat
463 = 337+125
674 = 337+337  = two beats
799 = 674+125
```

There is **no peak at 168 ms** (half of 337) — across all 528 gaps of segment A,
**exactly one** falls in 158–178 ms (167.2 ms, 0.19 %), and it is absorbed into the
211 ms basin. The right hand essentially never plays a straight eighth here. The
beat is split **1.70 : 1**, which is a human shuffle: between straight (1.00) and
a written triplet (2.00), closer to the triplet.

This is the whole ragged-figures problem, stated in one number. On the app's
display grid (semicorchea = 84.27 ms at 178 BPM) the swing pair falls at:

| gap | in semicorcheas | in semifusas (raw grid) |
|---|---|---|
| 124.6 ms | **1.479** | 5.91 |
| 211.3 ms | **2.507** | 10.03 |
| 337.2 ms | 4.001 | 16.01 |
| 674.2 ms | 8.001 | 32.00 |

The beat multiples land dead on. The swing pair lands **exactly halfway between
two columns** — 1.479 and 2.507 are as close to a coin toss as arithmetic allows.
38 % of segment A's gaps sit in that outer half (`fig6_binary_grid_phase.png`
shows the bimodality directly: one mass at 0, another at 1.0, nothing in between).
Which side each one rounds to is decided by noise, and *that* is what prints as an
unstable mix of corcheas and semicorcheas.

Two consequences worth stating plainly:

- **This is not a transcription error and not a sampling-resolution error.** The
  raw semifusa grid holds the pair fine (10 and 6 units of 16 per beat). Moving
  RAW_GRANULARITY finer cannot help, which matches what was measured when fusa →
  semifusa changed the span histogram from `{1:17, 2:7}` to `{1:18, 2:6}`.
- **No power-of-two subdivision can ever hold it.** A 5:3 split of the beat is
  not expressible as a pair of standard figures. It needs either a triplet
  reading (2:1, printable, 15 % off what was played) or an explicit swing
  annotation. That is a notation-model decision, not a quantiser tuning knob.

### 4a-bis. Worked example — the F–E–C at 00:46 (`fig7_worked_example.png`)

David's objection, and it is the right one to raise: *the sheet writes these as
corcheas, so if 337 ms is the negra, why is there no peak at 168 ms?*

The passage is F5 → E5 → C5 at 46.625 / 46.752 / 46.963 s on the video clock
(41.625 / 41.752 / 41.963 in the segment). Anchoring the beat grid on a
confidently on-beat attack at 42.087 s, every attack in the surrounding four
seconds sits either **on the beat** or at **63 % through it** — never at 50 %:

| attack | gap to previous | position within the beat |
|---|---|---|
| 40.399 E5 | 332 ms | 99.4 % (on the beat) |
| 40.614 F5+C5+A4 | 215 ms | 63.3 % |
| 41.625 **F5** | 1011 ms | 63.1 % |
| 41.752 **E5** | **127 ms** | 0.8 % (on the beat) |
| 41.963 **C5** | **210 ms** | 63.2 % |
| 42.087 C4 | 124 ms | 0.1 % (on the beat) |
| 42.417 chord | 330 ms | 98.0 % |

So the answer is: **a corchea in this piece is not 168 ms.** It is 211 ms when it
falls on the beat and 125 ms when it falls off it. 211 + 125 = 336 ≈ one negra, so
two corcheas still make a negra — they are just not equal to each other. 168 ms is
the *average* of the pair, and nobody plays the average. That is precisely why
there is no peak there, and it is the expected signature of a shuffle rather than
a contradiction.

The same picture shows what it costs. Read back from the stored score, the app
wrote this passage as **semicorchea → corchea con puntillo → semicorchea**
(1, 3 and 1 semicorchea columns) where the sheet says three equal corcheas
(2, 2, 2). Not one of the three is right, and the error is not random noise — it
is the deterministic consequence of rounding 1.48 and 2.51 columns to whole
numbers.

### 4b. The tempo change is real, and visible without any musical input

`fig4_sliding_unit.png` is the load-bearing picture. A 12 s window slides across
the piece and the fitted grid unit is plotted. For **200 seconds it is flat at
113.0 ms** — over 8–211 s the estimate stays inside 111.75–113.75 ms and moves by
at most **0.5 ms from one window to the next**. Then it steps up.

Be careful about what happens *after* the step, though: the post-change line is
**not** a second flat plateau. It settles near 117–118 ms for ~20 s, drifts to
125 ms around 240 s, then sits near 108 ms from 254 s on — and that last stretch
contains the densest windows in the piece (55–75 gaps each), so it is not a
low-count artifact. Segment B is genuinely less settled than segment A, which is
the same fact §4c reports from the other direction.

- The step happens in the window centred at **212 s** (covering 206–218 s).
- An independent split search (`out/table_split_search.csv`) — try every boundary
  from 180 to 255 s and keep the one whose two halves are jointly best explained
  by their own grid units — puts its best honest cluster at **215–218 s**, with
  **217 s tied for the top of it** (joint fit 0.69802 at 215, 216, 217 and 218 s).
  The nominal global maximum is at 245 s (0.69848), which is an artifact: past
  ~240 s the second half is short enough that almost any unit fits it.
- **The data agrees with the ear**, to the second.

The magnitude is a ~4.5 % slowdown of the underlying unit (113.0 → 118.5 ms).

Three spikes interrupt the flat line (≈8 s, ≈140–148 s, ≈250 s). They are **not
noise and not a bug**. Those windows contain no subdivisions at all — the
140–148 s window's fourteen gaps are 335, 339, 340, 342, 673, 674, 675, 1006,
1346, 1348, 1348, 1349, 1351, 2695 ms, i.e. pure beat multiples. With no fast
notes played, the tatum is simply not observable, and the largest unit that
explains the window is half the beat (169 ms) rather than a third. A production
version needs a continuity prior across windows; the honest raw output is shown
here instead.

### 4c. Segment B: the method finds the change but cannot name the beat

B's dominant peak (115 ms) is the *fastest* level, not the beat — in a running
passage the beat itself is often never struck. The beat objective produces a
near-tie:

| rank | beat | BPM | score | vs best | reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 697.5 ms | 86.0 | 0.798 | 100 % | compound — 115 ms is a triplet semicorchea (⅙) |
| 2 | 466.5 ms | 128.6 | 0.797 | **99.9 %** | binary — 115 ms is a plain semicorchea (¼) |
| 3 | 932.0 ms | 64.4 | 0.792 | 99.2 % | ×2 of rank 2 |
| 4 | 349.0 ms | 171.9 | 0.777 | 97.4 % | "same tempo as A, slightly slower" |

0.1 % is not a decision. The lower panel of `fig3_ratio_ladder.png` shows why the
runner-up is arguably the better musical reading: at 466.5 ms the peaks land on
**¼ (115), ½ (233), 1 (474), 2 (936)** — a clean binary ladder — whereas segment A
at its own beat lands on the *swung* ratios. If that reading is right, the change
at 3:37 is not merely a tempo change; it is a **change of subdivision scheme**,
from a shuffled compound feel to straight binary.

This is the method's real limit, and it is a structural one: **interval
statistics determine the grid up to a rational factor, never absolutely.** A beat
and twice that beat explain the same gaps equally well. Resolving it needs
information this analysis does not look at — accent, bar length, the left hand,
or a human picking from a shortlist. Which is exactly the interaction David
described: the machine finds the ladder, the user names the rungs.

Three smaller caveats on B, all pointing the same way:

- **189 intervals is thin** — A has 528.
- **B's dominant peak is much broader than A's.** The 44.4 % figure comes from a
  basin spanning 82–181 ms; only **21.7 %** of B's gaps lie within ±8 % of 115.4 ms,
  against **49.2 %** for A's 337 ms peak at the same tolerance. Side by side in the
  summary table the two "dominant shares" look comparable. They are not — A's is a
  spike, B's is a hill. The same applies to B's 88.4 % "mass inside a peak": at
  ±8 % it drops to 61 %, while A's holds up.
- **The 66 ms peak (11.6 %) fits nothing well** — 23 % off the nearest printable
  ratio. It is either ornamentation or transcription noise and deserves a look at
  the audio before being trusted.

## 5. Robustness

The 20 ms chord window is not a knob anyone has to defend — **anything from 10 ms
to 50 ms gives the same answer**:

| window | attacks | gaps < 50 ms | dominant peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 ms | 1413 | 695 | 335.6 ms |
| 5 ms | 763 | 45 | 336.7 ms |
| 10 ms | 721 | 3 | 337.2 ms |
| **20 ms** | **719** | **1** | **337.2 ms** |
| 30 ms | 719 | 1 | 337.2 ms |
| 50 ms | 718 | 0 | 337.2 ms |

Collapsing matters enormously (1413 → 719 attacks; 695 sub-50 ms gaps become 1).
*Where exactly* the threshold sits does not. Between 15 and 40 ms the attack list
is byte-identical.

## 6. Verdict

**Promising, with one named limitation.**

What it delivers:

- 92 % of segment A's gaps inside six peaks, median 1.2 % from a printable figure,
  with no BPM, no note names and no meter supplied.
- A tempo change located to within a few seconds of where it is heard, from a
  flat-line statistic that moves less than 0.5 ms over 200 s of music.
- A precise, falsifiable diagnosis of why the current pipeline produces ragged
  figures on this piece: a 1.70 : 1 swing landing at phase 0.48–0.51 on a binary
  display grid.

What it cannot do:

- Choose between a beat and its rational multiples. That needs a second signal or
  a human. Build the shortlist into the UI rather than pretending to a single
  answer.
- Say anything useful about a window with no subdivisions in it. Needs a
  continuity prior across windows.
- Deliver a *stable* estimate everywhere. Segment A is flat to half a
  millisecond; segment B drifts across 107–126 ms. Any consumer of this needs a
  confidence number, not just a value.

### Independently verified

Every headline number above was re-derived from `data/` by a second pass writing
its own code from scratch, without reading `scripts/`. The bijection, the 719
attacks, the 528/189 split, the six segment-A clusters, the 1.70 : 1 swing, the
1.479/2.507 phase, the flat 200 s and the 136–150 s explanation all reproduced.
That pass is also where the three corrections in this document came from: the
50 ms row of §5, the single 167 ms gap in §4a, and the "steps to ~118 and stays
there" overstatement in §4b. It further noted that the event↔matrix match is a
round-trip check on the *encoding*, not evidence that the hand split is musically
right — that question is out of scope here.

Suggested next spike: run the same measurement on the **left hand** and on a
piece with a known-clean binary feel. If the left hand independently reports
113.0 ms and the same step at ~212 s, the estimate is a property of the
performance rather than of the hand split — and that is the point at which it is
worth wiring into the tempo map.
